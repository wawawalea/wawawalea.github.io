About

I am a senior at New York University, with a double major in Computer Science and Philosophy. I am very fortunate to work with Prof. Xinpeng Wang on safety evaluation of large language models.

I am interested in two things about large language models.

  1. Evaluation methods that reflect the behavior of a model in real deployment.
  2. Methods to interpret and control a model, so that the law and society are better prepared for the tool.

AI safety

The work gathered under the name of AI safety differs first in the source of the danger it considers. In one body of work the danger is an adversary: the model does as its makers intended until someone sets out to break it, with a crafted prompt or with poisoned data. In another the danger lies in the system built around the model, in the way a product handles the data and the permissions of its users. In a third the danger is the model itself. Nobody attacks it and nobody misuses it. The property that causes harm was acquired in training and appears in ordinary use.

My work belongs to the third. I came to safety from post-training, and my worries about language models were formed while training them. Anyone who has trained a model with reinforcement learning has seen it acquire behavior that nobody specified, and has reason to doubt that an evaluation at the end will find all of it.

That doubt is the subject of my current project. A safety evaluation is meant to bound the behavior of a model in deployment. As models grow more capable they also grow better at telling an evaluation from deployment, and there is not yet a measure of the effect this has on a given result. With my advisor, I am working toward one, for agentic tasks at the center of safety concern.

The same concern reaches further than evaluation awareness. Training can leave a model with rule violations conditional on being unobserved, with deliberate underperformance on tests of capability, with written reasoning that no longer reflects the computation behind an answer. None of them requires an adversary, and all of them weaken the evidence on which a model is judged safe. I expect to stay in this part of AI safety.

AI policy

The work gathered under the name of AI policy differs in the role it gives to the law. Some of it takes the law as a standard and tests whether a system meets it. Some takes the law as a list of requirements and builds the technical means to satisfy them: methods for unlearning, for watermarking, for tracing an output to its training data. Some measures models so that those who write rules have facts to write from. Some designs the rules and institutions that do not yet exist. And some examines the law itself.

My interest began with the last. Most of the law that will govern language models was written before them. A statute carries assumptions about the thing it regulates, and some of those assumptions do not hold for a language model. When one fails, the legal concept built on it loses its reference, and it has to be given a definition under which it can be measured again.

Data protection law is the place I have looked first. The right to erasure assumes that personal data sits in a place from which it can be removed. A model trained on that data holds it in its weights, in no single place and as a matter of degree. The law also treats sensitive information more strictly than the rest, on the assumption that the two can be kept apart, and a capable model can infer the first from enough of the second. My current project measures personal information in a model with a public pre-training corpus: the facts it holds about a person, and the inferences it draws from them.

Evaluation awareness can be read the same way. A rule that admits a model to use on the strength of a test assumes that the model behaves in use as it did under test.

I expect privacy to remain at the center of this work, and I expect the same question to arise in other bodies of law. The law of product safety and liability carries its own assumptions about the things it regulates, and it will be applied to language models. Nor do I mean to stay with one kind of policy work. Building the means a law requires, and supplying the measurements a rule-maker needs, are both tasks for someone trained in machine learning. The field has introduced a new tool into society, and it owes society the means to regulate it.
