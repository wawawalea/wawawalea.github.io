When studying a machine learning problem, I like to ask:

1. What is the object of study?
Before I measure or optimize something, I ask what it actually is.

Often the real question is whether the thing we probe is a single object at all, or a loose set of case-specific cues.

2. How does it work?
Once the object is clear, I ask what produces it.

I want the mechanism and the learning dynamics behind a behavior, not just whether a metric moves.

3. How far does it transfer?
A result on one setting is not a general result. I ask what transfers, and where it breaks.

In continual and post-training work, the question is not whether something changed. It is how far the change transfers across tasks, models, and distributions.

All the Isms in Mechanistic Interpretability

A decision tree of the metaphysical positions that shape mechanistic interpretability in 2026.

August 7, 2026

Mechanistic interpretability makes claims about what exists inside a model. The field asks what counts as a feature. The field also asks when two circuits count as the same mechanism and what an intervention can prove.

The tree below makes these commitments explicit. The positions are not exclusive schools. A researcher may adopt different positions for different goals.

The central disagreement

The central disagreement is not whether researchers should use SAEs or neurons. The disagreement concerns what those methods can discover.

Feature realism treats a decomposition as a candidate inventory of real internal parts. Pragmatic pluralism treats a decomposition as a tool chosen for a task. Other positions change the success criterion from accurate description to statistical stability, formal proof, audit value, or a redesigned model.
